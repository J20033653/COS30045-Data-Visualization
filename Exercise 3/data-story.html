Which TV Should You Buy If You Care About Your Power Bill?

This page tells the story behind our television energy consumption dataset for three audiences: consumers shopping for an energy-efficient TV, policy makers tracking household energy trends, and researchers studying efficiency in consumer electronics. Two questions matter most to them: does a bigger screen always mean higher running costs, and does the Australian Energy Rating star label actually predict lower energy use?

1. Bigger screens use more power — but technology matters too

The chart below plots each television's screen size against its estimated annual energy consumption, grouped by panel technology (LED, QLED, OLED). As expected, energy use climbs steadily with screen size. But at the same size, the technology used makes a real difference — OLED panels in this dataset tend to sit at the higher end of energy use for their size, while LED panels are generally the most efficient. For a size-conscious, budget-conscious buyer, this means the panel type is just as important a decision as the number of inches on the box.

2. The star rating is a reliable shortcut

Rather than comparing raw kWh figures across dozens of models, most shoppers rely on the Australian Energy Rating star label. The chart below groups our dataset by star rating and shows the average annual energy use for each group. The relationship is clear and consistent: every half-star increase corresponds to a meaningful drop in average annual energy use. This confirms the star rating is a trustworthy, low-effort signal for consumers who don't want to dig into technical specifications — and a useful benchmark for policy makers assessing whether the labelling scheme is doing its job.

Take-away

For Consumers

Check the star rating first, then the panel technology. A smaller, higher-rated LED TV can cost noticeably less to run each year than a larger OLED of the same star band.

For Policy Makers

The star rating scheme shows a clear, consistent relationship with energy use in this sample, supporting its continued use as a household energy-reduction tool.

For Researchers

Screen size alone is not a sufficient predictor of energy use; panel technology is a confounding factor worth controlling for in further analysis.
